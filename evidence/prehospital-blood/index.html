Prehospital blood: state of evidence

Trial data, utilization figures, and current guidance. Prose commentary to follow.

Reference page Compiled 2026-08-24 4 references unverified

Two distinct questions are usually discussed as one:

  • Does prehospital transfusion improve outcomes over standard care?
  • Which product should be transfused, once that decision is made?

They have different evidence bases and different answers. Neither has been studied in roughly half of the patients who receive blood from ground ambulances.

The product question: whole blood against components

  • Two randomized trials reported in 2026. Both null.
  • Neither found a mortality advantage for whole blood over components at up to two units.
  • TOWAR's point estimate favors components, but the confidence interval crosses one. This is failure to demonstrate benefit, not demonstration of harm. The interval also does not exclude a meaningful mortality increase.
  • TOWAR's cluster-randomized two-to-one allocation produced markedly unequal group sizes.
  • The TOWAR storage age substudy was null through 21 days, adjusted odds ratio 0.99 (95% CI 0.74 to 1.32). The signal reported in earlier smaller work did not replicate.
  • The accompanying New England Journal of Medicine editorial treated the two trials as consistent.

The prior question: transfusion against standard care

  • Evidence is discordant and predates both 2026 trials.
  • The most plausible reconciliation is transport interval. Post-hoc analysis of PAMPer and COMBAT located the survival benefit in patients with transport times exceeding 20 minutes.
  • Populations differed substantially across the three trials, particularly injury severity, penetrating mechanism, and time to emergency department arrival.
  • Any rural or long-transport service reading the null results as applying to its own operating conditions should check those conditions against the trial populations first.

What the trials do not cover

  • Both 2026 trials capped the intervention at two units. Whether whole blood outperforms components in patients requiring massive transfusion is untested.
  • TROOP is enrolling roughly 1,100 severely injured adults at 15 US trauma centers, primary outcome 6-hour mortality, expected to complete around mid-2027.
  • All randomized evidence is trauma. No trial has enrolled a nontraumatic hemorrhage patient.

Ground utilization

  • More than 48,000 emergency medical services (EMS) transfusions were documented nationally across 2024 to 2025, including more than 22,000 on ground ambulance responses.
  • Whole blood accounted for approximately 62% of documented ground transfusions and 23% of transfusions overall.
  • Ground-based transfusion as a share of all prehospital transfusion doubled from 5% to 10% between 2023 and 2025.
  • The Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition passed 400 participating 911 ground services in June 2026.
  • Across systems whose protocols permit nontraumatic transfusion, roughly 40% to 46% of product goes to patients no trial has enrolled.

Outcomes in nontraumatic hemorrhage

  • Entirely retrospective. No randomized data.
  • Transfusion measurably improves the parameters it is expected to improve. No study has demonstrated an outcome difference.
  • Only one series includes an untransfused comparison group, and that series found no difference in mortality, intensive care days, length of stay, or rate of intervention.
  • Surrogate improvement in shock resuscitation has a long record of not translating to survival.

Why the trauma data does not extrapolate

  • Comparator mismatch. TOWAR and SWiFT compared whole blood against components, with both arms transfused. In nontraumatic practice the real-world comparator is crystalloid or nothing. The trials answer a product-selection question this population has not reached.
  • The nontraumatic patient is typically older, frequently chronically anemic, and often coagulopathic from hepatic disease or anticoagulation rather than acute trauma-induced coagulopathy.
  • No tissue injury drives the inflammatory and endothelial response that damage control resuscitation is designed to interrupt.
  • Definitive hemorrhage control is endoscopic or interventional rather than surgical, with different time constants.
  • Extrapolation may still be correct. It is currently an assumption carrying 40% to 46% of ground whole blood utilization.

Current guidance

All three governing documents are scoped to trauma. All predate TOWAR and SWiFT.

  • 2023 joint position statement (American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma, American College of Emergency Physicians, National Association of EMS Physicians). Patients with signs of hemorrhagic shock should receive prehospital blood products whenever available. Whole blood is preferred over packed red cells; if only components are available, transfuse one-to-one. Supporting claim is a 37% reduction in 30-day mortality from observational literature. Listed indications are trauma-specific throughout. No nontraumatic indication appears anywhere in the document.
  • Treatment specifics from the same statement: one gram calcium gluconate per one to two units transfused, on the basis that prehospital transfusion in civilian trauma is associated with hypocalcemia which itself predicts decreased survival and massive transfusion requirement. Transfusion through a blood warmer to a delivery temperature of 38 degrees Celsius, not exceeding 42 degrees.
  • 2025 NAEMSP Prehospital Trauma Compendium on transfusion. Blood components over crystalloid as first-line for traumatic life-threatening bleeding. Low titer group O whole blood as first-choice product, conditioned on systems able to support a high-quality transfusion program. States explicitly that the impact of blood administration on patient outcomes remains poorly defined, with significant heterogeneity in the quality of supporting literature.
  • 2025 Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition clinical practice guideline for civilian EMS.

The 2026 trials did not contradict the guidance authors. They sharpened a limitation the 2025 compendium had already named.

The data problem underneath

  • No regional, state, or national systems exist for collecting outcome data on prehospital transfusion.
  • Hospital reporting of outcomes back to EMS agencies is inconsistent.
  • Trauma centers have raised concerns that incorporating agency data into trauma registries risks violating the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.
  • Many nontraumatic patients are transported to hospitals with no trauma designation and therefore no registry.
  • No accepted method exists for collecting data on nontraumatic hemorrhage specifically.
  • This is fixable at agency level. A program capturing its own outcomes prospectively, including nontraumatic recipients, is producing data that does not currently exist.
