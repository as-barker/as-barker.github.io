Prehospital Blood: Work in Progress Review

Working document, still fleshing this out.

Work in progressUpdated 2026-08-25

The 2026 trials did not test whether prehospital blood works. They tested which blood product works best.

  • TOWAR and SWiFT both published null. Both are being read as a verdict on the practice.
  • Neither had an arm that received no blood.
  • Programs are being argued against right now on the strength of that misread.

The ceiling on the question

The question worth answering cannot be asked, and understanding why is the precondition for reading any of this literature correctly.

  • The clean question: does prehospital blood improve survival against no blood?
  • Every patient in every one of these trials receives blood, on arrival to the ED at the latest.
  • Randomizing a bleeding patient to withhold an indicated product sitting on the aircraft does not clear ethical review, and it should not.
  • What remains testable is much narrower: does moving transfusion twenty to forty minutes earlier change the outcome?
  • That is a far smaller effect to detect than "does transfusion work?" It explains most of what these trials found.
  • Absence of a detected difference in a narrow question is not evidence against the broad one.

The Evidence

RePHILL 2022 UK, n=432 RBCs + lyophilized plasma vs. saline Episode mortality or failure of lactate clearance No difference
PAMPer 2018 US air medical, n=501 2 U thawed plasma vs. standard care 30-day mortality Benefit (23.2% vs. 33.0%)
COMBAT 2018 Denver urban ground, n=144 2 U plasma vs. saline 28-day mortality No benefit, stopped early

The 2026 trials

  • TOWAR's 30-day mortality ran numerically higher in the whole blood arm, not statistically significant, with no difference in any secondary outcome.
  • TOWAR also tested storage age, 1 to 14 days against 15 to 21. Nothing. That has direct operational value for how you rotate and what you waste.
  • SWiFT randomized 942 and analyzed 616 after excluding nontraumatic hemorrhage and traumatic arrest. Mortality equal or numerically higher in the whole blood arm at every timepoint out to 90 days. PT above normal more often in that group. Serious adverse events more common in standard care.
  • Both capped prehospital transfusion at two units. Roughly three quarters of patients received four units or fewer in 24 hours, and most reverted to components on arrival.
  • TOWAR's confidence interval spans both meaningful benefit and meaningful harm. That reads as cannot tell, not as the same.

The plasma split

  • PAMPer enrolled air medical patients with long transports and showed benefit. COMBAT enrolled urban ground patients with short transports and showed nothing.
  • Pooled analysis, JAMA Surgery 2020: the plasma benefit concentrated in patients with transport times over twenty minutes.
  • That finding is post hoc and I will not pretend otherwise. It is still the only coherent account anyone has offered for why two trials of the same intervention disagreed, and it points at time rather than product.

What the case for early transfusion rests on

The case never rested on these trials, which is why these trials do not disturb it.

  • The physiology is not in dispute. Hemorrhagic shock kills through failed oxygen delivery and progressive coagulopathy. Blood corrects both. Crystalloid corrects neither and worsens the second.
  • Far-forward and en-route transfusion is established military doctrine, built on MEDEVAC data showing improved 24-hour and 30-day survival. Observational, young, penetrating, and not transferable line for line to civilian EMS. But that is where the practice came from and it was not adopted on a hunch.
  • Sperry's own framing of his own null result: the PAMPer control arm, on identical vital sign thresholds and inclusion criteria, ran about 33% 30-day mortality. TOWAR and contemporary cohorts run around 24%. Very few air services carried red cells in 2018 and nearly all carry something now. His attribution is to blood being aboard at all rather than to which blood is aboard.

Eight years apart, different systems, different in-hospital care, different TXA uptake. It is hypothesis-generating and not much else. But it is directionally consistent with PAMPer, with the transport-time analysis, and with the military experience, and it is the argument being made by the man who ran the trial that supposedly undercuts it.

The population no one has studied

Nearly half of EMS-administered blood goes to medical causes of hemorrhage. Nobody has published a mortality outcome in that population.

  • 2024 nationwide survey of agencies with active programs: 42% of prehospital transfusion indications were medical or non-trauma, with GI bleeding the most common indication in geriatric patients.
  • Same survey: 68% of agencies reported up to a quarter of their transfusions going to women with pregnancy-related hemorrhage.
  • Finnish ground EMS program: 45% nontraumatic, GI hemorrhage second only to blunt trauma.
  • US Northeast cohort ran roughly 28% non-trauma against 9% in a comparable Swedish cohort. Adoption patterns diverge sharply by system.
  • UK air ambulance series, 50 nontraumatic patients: shock index improved from 1.39 to 0.97 after transfusion.
  • What exists is feasibility, safety, and physiologic response.
  • What does not exist is a single mortality outcome study in prehospital nontraumatic hemorrhage. Not one.
  • SWiFT excluded these patients by design. TOWAR was a trauma trial.

The randomized evidence base everyone is citing excludes the population that now accounts for close to half of actual use. Any deeper review should address that, and most of the commentary I have seen doesn't.

What is still unsettled

  • Whether two units was ever a large enough dose to show a difference.
  • Whether selection and time to first unit matter more than product choice.
  • Whether the twenty-minute transport threshold from the plasma trials transfers to whole blood.
  • What outcomes look like in GI, obstetric, and vascular hemorrhage.
  • The right role for freeze-dried and lyophilized plasma in systems that cannot stock whole blood.

Where the guidelines sit

Every guideline currently in play was written before the 2026 trials landed.

Source Year Position
NAEMSP, Prehospital Trauma Compendium 2025 Components over crystalloid for traumatic life-threatening bleeding. LTOWB as first-choice product, conditional on a system that can support a high-quality program.
Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition 2025 Operational guidance for civilian EMS: transfusion criteria, permissive hypotension targets, program structure.
European Trauma Guideline, 6th ed. 2023 Insufficient evidence to recommend for or against prehospital blood products.

Expect the product-preference language to move. Nothing here touches the recommendation to transfuse rather than run crystalloid.

The practical read

The choice between whole blood and components is a logistics decision now. We should treat it as one.

  • If you run an LTOWB program: nothing here says stop. These trials found no advantage over components, not harm. The logistical case, one product, no thawing, a simpler workflow, less wastage under a two-person crew working a scene, was never what they measured and survives them intact.
  • If you are building one: the case for whole blood specifically is operational now rather than clinical. That is still a good case. Make it honestly, because the people across the table have read the headlines.
  • If you are choosing between the two: choose on logistics, supply relationship, and wastage.
  • What deserves the attention instead is patient selection, time to first unit, and whether you are capturing outcomes on your medical hemorrhage patients at all.

Every one of those unstudied patients is a data point somebody could have collected. That gap does not close from the top down. It closes because programs decide to write it down.
